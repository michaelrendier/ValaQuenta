The detector Δ(w) = w·𝟏: zero exactly on the nilpotents

For a word w and the all-ones word 𝟏, w·𝟏 = 0 if and only if w² = 0. 𝟏 itself is not an annihilator: e₀·𝟏 = 𝟏. A picture shows a structure; it is not evidence that the structure means anything beyond its definition.

About this visual

What you are looking at. Two ways of asking whether a word is nilpotent, side by side. Squaring asks directly (w·w = 0?). The detector Δ(w) = w·𝟏 multiplies by the all-ones word 𝟏 instead (every bit set), the trace-Laplacian test of the ring-theory spine.

How to read it. The upper card has one cell per word of d = 8. Row 1 is coloured by the class of w² (blue 0, orange e₀). Row 2 is coloured by whether Δ(w) is zero (blue) or not (orange). Row 3 is green where the two rows agree. Hover for the numbers. In the calculator, type a word in decimal, 0x… or 0b…, choose d, and read w², w·𝟏 and the verdict; the last button tests 20,000 random 32-bit words in your browser.

What it shows. Δ(w) = 0 if and only if w² = 0: rows 1 and 2 are the same pattern and row 3 is solid green. Both are the parity of the number of 1 bits: w² = parity·e₀ and w·𝟏 = parity·𝟏, so the calculator also prints the closed form and checks it for your word. The same agreement holds for every word at d = 2, 4, 8 (exhaustive) and for 20,000 random words at d = 32 (the Python test found no exception; the button runs a fresh sample).

What it does not show. That the detector is anything deeper than the weight parity (it is that); and that 𝟏 annihilates everything: it does not. e₀·𝟏 = 𝟏 ≠ 0, which the calculator prints for every word and dimension. An earlier lemma (the UDEO white paper) claimed 𝟏 was a global annihilator; it is retracted (2026-08-22). This page demonstrates the corrected statement, and it proves nothing for d = 32 beyond the sample.

Numbers to check. d = 8, w = 0x6B: w² = 0x01 (involutory), Δ(w) = 0xFF (not zero), so 'iff' holds. The 20,000-word test at d = 32 reports class counts near 10,000 and 10,000 and 0 mismatches.
