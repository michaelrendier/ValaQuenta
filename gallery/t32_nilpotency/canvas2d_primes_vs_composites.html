Primes against composites, by decimal length

Is the nilpotency of the Hyperwebster word special for primes? By length of the integer: the two-digit table, and where the excess goes. A picture shows a structure; it is not evidence that the structure means anything beyond its definition.

About this visual

What you are looking at. The question 'are primes special for this algebra?' asked by length. For every integer n the Hyperwebster word of n is a 32-bit word (page 3), and it is nilpotent exactly when it has an even number of 1 bits. Here n runs through every integer of one decimal length, and primes are compared with composites, and with the composites that end in 1, 3, 7 or 9 (the last digits a prime above 5 can have).

How to read it. Card 1: for each decimal length (1…5 digits) two bars, primes and composites, with the 50% line; counts under each bar. Card 2: the 90 two-digit numbers as a table, tens digit down, last digit across; blue = nilpotent (even number of 1 bits), orange = not; a ring marks a prime. Hover for n, its address, its bits, its weight. Card 3: any window [lo, hi) up to 99,999, computed in your browser, with the one-sided hypergeometric P that primes are at least as nilpotent as they are by chance among the same numbers.

What it shows. Nilpotency here is the Thue–Morse sequence (the parity of the binary digit sum) of the address, so the expected rate is ½ for any set of numbers with no relation to binary digits; at 3, 4 and 5 digits primes and composites both sit at 49–51%. The excess is confined to the two-digit table: 17 of 21 primes against 29 of 69 composites, P = 0.0016 for that one table. Published work on the binary digit sum of primes (Mauduit and Rivat 2010, Post-Hoc) finds no bias; the address here is not the prime itself, so that is a parallel, not a proof.

What it does not show. That two-digit primes are special: the table was looked at because the 16 sedenion basis primes and the 15 Moonshine primes are small, so P = 0.0016 is a look-elsewhere number (several windows were examined), and the effect is gone by three digits. It does not show why three digits wash it out. One reading offered by the author is that three digits are needed to carry the three-part structure (inclination, declination, distance; Add:Scale:Sign); the data are consistent with that and do not test it.

Numbers to check. 2…9: 2 of 4 primes. 10…99: primes 17/21 = 81%, composites 29/69 = 42%, composites ending 1,3,7,9 4/15 = 27%, P = 0.0016. 100…999: primes 70/143 = 49%, composites 377/757 = 50%. 1,000…9,999: 49% and 50%. 10,000…99,999: 51% and 50%. Each is recomputed in your browser and compared with Python.
