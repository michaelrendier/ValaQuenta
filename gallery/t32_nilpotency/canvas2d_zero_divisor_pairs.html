Zero-divisor pairs: a·b = 0 with a and b both nonzero

The matrix of the T32/GF(2) algebra: cell (a, b) is lit when a ≠ 0, b ≠ 0 and a·b = 0. Over GF(2) this algebra is commutative at d = 2, 4, 8 (checked exhaustively), so a·b = 0 exactly when b·a = 0 and the matrix is symmetric; the colours would show a difference if one existed. A picture shows a structure; it is not evidence that the structure means anything beyond its definition.

About this visual

What you are looking at. Every ordered pair (a, b) of nonzero words of the algebra whose product is zero. Such a pair is a pair of zero divisors; in ordinary arithmetic there are none, and the Cayley–Dickson algebras acquire them at the sedenions. Row = a, column = b.

How to read it. A cell is lit when a·b = 0 (a, b nonzero). Blue: both a·b and b·a are zero. Orange would mean a·b = 0 only, purple b·a = 0 only. Hover for the two products; tap a row to list the partners of that word. Switch d to 2, 4 or 8.

What it shows. The count of zero-divisor pairs: 1 at d = 2, 25 at d = 4, 2,689 at d = 8 (the Python engine enumerates the same). Only blue ever appears: over GF(2) the algebra is commutative at these dimensions (checked exhaustively; the engine's test records it), so a·b = 0 exactly when b·a = 0 and the picture is symmetric. The visible lattice is the structure of the annihilators.

What it does not show. Anything about the 84 sedenion zero-divisors of the box-kite engine (those live in the real sedenions; this algebra is over GF(2) and its pairs are a different set). It also does not say how the pairs are organised beyond the picture (no box-kite or assessor structure is claimed here). At d = 16 the matrix would have 4.3 billion cells and is not drawn.

Numbers to check. d = 2: 1 pair; d = 4: 25; d = 8: 2,689 of 65,025 candidate pairs. The line under the matrix compares with Python.
