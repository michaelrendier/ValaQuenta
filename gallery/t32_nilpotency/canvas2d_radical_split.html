The square law: every word is nilpotent or an involutory unit

Every word w of the T32/GF(2) algebra of dimension d has w² = 0 or w² = e₀; nothing else occurs. At d = 8 the 256 words split 128 | 128. A picture shows a structure; it is not evidence that the structure means anything beyond its definition.

About this visual

What you are looking at. Every word of the algebra, one cell each. The algebra is T32/GF(2): words are d-bit integers (d = 2, 4, 8, 16 here; 32 in the engine), multiplied by the Cayley–Dickson doubling rule carried out over GF(2), the field with two elements. This is the construction that gives ℝ → ℂ → ℍ → 𝕆 → 𝕊 over the reals, one doubling further and with XOR for addition.

How to read it. A cell is one word w = row·side + column (or its place in the class-grouped order). Blue: w·w = 0, a nilpotent word (the zero word counts, as the ring's radical includes 0). Orange: w·w = e₀, an involutory unit (e₀ is the word 1, the multiplicative identity). Hover or tap a cell for w in hex, decimal and bits, w², and the detector Δ(w) = w·𝟏.

What it shows. The square law: for every word, w² is 0 or e₀, and nothing else ever occurs. The algebra therefore splits into two halves, the radical and the involutory units, 2^(d−1) words each: 2 | 2, 8 | 8, 128 | 128 and 32,768 | 32,768 at d = 2, 4, 8, 16. The grouped view puts the two halves side by side.

What it does not show. That the split is a law of the algebra for all d: it is verified here exhaustively for d ≤ 16 and by sampling at d = 32, not proved. It does not say which words are in which half by any simple rule on the bits (the by-value picture has structure but no closed form here). It says nothing about primes, hashes or anything outside the algebra.

Numbers to check. d = 8: 128 nilpotent, 128 involutory, 0 other, matching the Python radical_census(8). d = 16: 32,768 and 32,768. The line under the grid compares with the Python census for d = 2, 4, 8.
